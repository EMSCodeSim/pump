1Role, responsibility & safetyWhat the engineer owns before the wheels moveNot complete

Lesson

The driver/operator is responsible for far more than steering. The job combines apparatus readiness, safe vehicle operation, crew awareness, positioning, communication, and sound judgment under time pressure.

Treat department SOPs, state law, manufacturer limits, seat-belt requirements, and scene safety practices as hard boundaries. Emergency response does not remove the obligation to operate with due regard for others.

Know this cold

  • Seat belts and crew readiness before movement
  • Apparatus dimensions, weight, turning radius and stopping limits
  • When to slow, stop or request a spotter
  • Difference between an urgent response and reckless driving
  • Accountability for apparatus condition and documented defects

Hands-on drill

  • Walk around your assigned apparatus and identify height, length, width, GVWR and major blind spots
  • Locate seat-belt indicators, warning devices, mirrors/cameras and emergency controls
  • Discuss three conditions that would make you slow or stop even during an emergency response

Learning objectives

  • Describe the driver/operator role
  • Identify major preventable driving hazards
  • Explain why SOP/manufacturer limits outrank habit
Knowledge check: Which principle is most important when response urgency conflicts with safe vehicle control?

2Apparatus inspection & readinessFind problems before the call finds themNot complete

Lesson

A disciplined inspection begins outside the cab and follows a repeatable pattern. Check fluids, tires/wheels, leaks, lights, brakes, steering, batteries, belts, warning systems, compartment security, water/agent levels, mounted equipment, and any system specific to the apparatus.

The goal is not to become a mechanic. The goal is to recognize unsafe or abnormal conditions, document them correctly, and know when the vehicle should not be placed in service.

Know this cold

  • Use the same walk-around sequence every time
  • Look under the apparatus before moving it
  • Verify compartment doors and equipment are secured
  • Know normal gauge indications and warning lamps
  • Document and communicate deficiencies

Hands-on drill

  • Complete your department check sheet without skipping around
  • Have an instructor plant two safe-to-simulate defects and identify them
  • Verbally report one defect using location, condition, operational impact and action taken

Learning objectives

  • Perform a systematic pre-trip
  • Recognize common out-of-service concerns
  • Use department inspection forms correctly

3Vehicle dynamics & defensive drivingHeavy apparatus do not drive like passenger carsNot complete

Lesson

Apparatus weight, center of gravity, water movement, tire loading, speed, road surface and grade all change stopping and cornering behavior. Smooth inputs preserve traction and stability.

Defensive driving means looking well ahead, maintaining space, anticipating traffic errors, controlling speed before a hazard, and always preserving an escape path.

Know this cold

  • Speed is a major contributor to rollover and stopping distance
  • Brake before the turn, not deep into it
  • Allow extra following distance and reaction space
  • Expect civilian drivers to behave unpredictably
  • Grades, curves, wet/icy roads and crosswinds demand earlier speed reduction

Hands-on drill

  • On a controlled course, compare smooth vs abrupt braking at instructor-selected safe speeds
  • Practice mirror scanning and verbal hazard callouts
  • Discuss the effect of a partially filled tank on handling

Learning objectives

  • Explain weight transfer and rollover risk
  • Apply defensive following and scanning
  • Adjust speed for road/weather/traffic
Knowledge check: When should most speed reduction for a curve occur?

4Road driving & response judgmentIntersections, lanes, grades and real-world trafficNot complete

Lesson

Road-route proficiency should include ordinary and emergency-response driving situations likely in your area: intersections, turns, lane changes, grades, narrow roads, railroad crossings, limited-access roads and low-clearance awareness.

Intersections deserve deliberate attention because other drivers may not see or hear the apparatus. Clear each conflict lane and be prepared to stop.

Know this cold

  • Read the road far ahead
  • Clear intersections lane by lane
  • Use warning devices as tools, not permission
  • Avoid outrunning visibility and stopping distance
  • Know local routes, bridges, grades and clearance hazards

Hands-on drill

  • Drive a predetermined non-emergency route with instructor scoring
  • Call out each intersection hazard and escape option
  • Identify at least three low-clearance, grade or tight-turn concerns in your first-due

Learning objectives

  • Demonstrate controlled route driving
  • Use deliberate intersection technique
  • Identify route-specific apparatus hazards
Knowledge check: At an intersection, what does a green light guarantee for an emergency vehicle?

5Backing, spotters & restricted spacesControl blind spots before they control youNot complete

Lesson

Backing incidents are highly preventable. Use a spotter when available and required by policy, establish signals before movement, stop immediately if visual contact is lost, and get out to look when the space is uncertain.

Restricted-space work is about slow, precise control. Mirror use, reference points, front-swing awareness and tire tracking matter more than speed.

Know this cold

  • Avoid backing when practical
  • Agree on hand/radio signals before moving
  • Stop when the spotter disappears from view
  • Know front swing and rear overhang
  • Use GOAL — Get Out And Look — when unsure

Hands-on drill

  • Practice right and left alley-dock style backing
  • Back to a stop point using a spotter and predetermined signals
  • Repeat one evolution with mirrors only if allowed by instructor/AHJ

Learning objectives

  • Use a spotter effectively
  • Back into a restricted space
  • Judge tire path and apparatus overhang
Knowledge check: What should the driver do immediately after losing sight of the assigned spotter while backing?
Complete the lesson, perform the drill, then mark the module complete.
6Cone-course masterySerpentine, turn-around and diminishing clearanceNot complete

Lesson

Cone courses test vehicle placement, mirror discipline, steering control and the ability to judge clearances. Learn the geometry instead of memorizing wheel turns from one apparatus.

Before the test, know the evaluation rules, boundary definitions, use of spotters, pull-forward limits and safety-failure criteria used by your AHJ.

Know this cold

  • Move at a controlled walking pace unless instructed otherwise
  • Set mirrors before starting
  • Use fixed reference points but adapt to apparatus dimensions
  • Watch both rear tracking and front swing
  • Never trade safety for a perfect time

Hands-on drill

  • Run serpentine forward and reverse
  • Perform a confined-space 180-degree turn
  • Run diminishing-clearance or lane-boundary exercise
  • Finish with an alley dock on both sides

Learning objectives

  • Complete restricted-space maneuvers
  • Judge horizontal and vertical clearance
  • Turn apparatus within confined space
Knowledge check: Why is memorizing a fixed number of steering-wheel turns a poor long-term strategy?

7Positioning & scene operationsArrive in a place that helps the incidentNot complete

Lesson

Good positioning protects crews, supports hose deployment and water supply, preserves access for later-arriving apparatus, and avoids collapse zones, wires, soft shoulders and other hazards.

Think beyond your own apparatus. Positioning should support the incident plan, aerial access, hydrant use, patient access and traffic control.

Know this cold

  • Protect the work area without blocking critical access
  • Avoid collapse zones and overhead hazards
  • Consider pump-panel exposure to traffic/fire
  • Leave room for aerials and special operations
  • Set brake/chocks/stabilization as required before work begins

Hands-on drill

  • Given three building photos or station locations, choose and defend an apparatus position
  • Walk around the parked apparatus and identify exposure/hose/traffic hazards
  • Practice safe roadway blocking setup per department policy

Learning objectives

  • Select safe functional apparatus position
  • Recognize scene-placement hazards
  • Coordinate position with incident needs
Knowledge check: Which positioning choice is best?

8DO1 practical & written prepTurn knowledge into a repeatable test-day routineNot complete

Lesson

Practical success comes from a consistent sequence: inspect, adjust, buckle, communicate, scan, move deliberately, and stop when uncertain. Speak critical safety actions aloud when the evaluator needs to observe your decision process.

For written preparation, prioritize vehicle dynamics, inspection, defensive driving, backing/spotters, positioning, apparatus limitations, policies and common safety decisions.

Know this cold

  • Know exactly what your AHJ evaluates
  • Practice in the apparatus you will test on when possible
  • Use verbal callouts for safety-critical actions
  • Treat every cone as an object that cannot be struck
  • Review mistakes immediately after each practice run
